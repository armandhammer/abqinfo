DOWNTOWN STATIONS	
[U]
Downtown (DT) stations are located within the urban core of 
downtown Albuquerque. Downtown ART service runs on parallel 
roads off of Central due to a narrow right-of-way. This loop includes 
more frequent stops than in other portions of the transit system due 
to the high concentration of destinations and future high concentra­
tion of housing and overall ridership. 
In many dense, historic downtowns, station location has relatively 
little effect on the surrounding blocks. However, in Albuquerque, 
downtown stations will permit increased development on adjacent 
blocks by significantly reducing parking demand in the core. The 
reduced parking demand is due to workers commuting in on transit 
as well as downtown residents commuting out on transit. 
While parking is not required for downtown uses, leasing office 
space, retail space, and renting housing without parking can be 
difficult in an automobile-oriented city. As transit service increases, 
however, the demand for parking should decrease. Without every­
thing needed for daily life within downtown at present, the land 
near transit stations is likely to be more valuable as a result, connect­
ing to services and housing outside of the district.
Increased development and activity will significantly increase pedes­
trian traffic throughout the downtown. While pedestrian consider­
ations are extremely important in all station areas, they are critical 
in downtown station areas. Considerations must be made for safe, 
comfortable, and convenient pedestrian movements, including:

URBAN CENTER STATIONS	
[U]
Urban Center (UC) stations are located in or near high-intensity 
mixed-use areas, often including cultural institutions. Urban Cen­
ters are less intense than downtowns but significantly more intense 
than neighborhood centers. Rio Grande, West Downtown, and 
Bryn Mawr, are Urban Centers, including a mix of commercial, resi­
dential, and institutional uses within close proximity.
Urban Centers are destinations, typically drawing people from other 
parts of the city. As such, they can drive transit ridership, especially 
over weekends. Many urban centers have the capacity for increased 
housing and should provide sufficient residential density to meet 
their potential.
Like Downtown Stations, walkable streets are key to the success of 
urban centers. Due to typically high densities within urban centers, 
they tend to provide most needed services within the center area 
and are most efficiently traversed on foot or by bicycle. If an urban 
center is primarily serviced by automobiles, it is likely not supplying 
sufficient housing or is missing key services like a grocery. Excep­
tions to this rule may occur with major institutions with regional 
draws, like museums.
URBAN NEIGHBORHOOD STATIONS	
[U]
Albuquerque currently lacks many urban neighborhoods but they 
may be aspirational in the future. At this time, Walter is the only 
Urban Neighborhood. Urban neighborhoods (UN) are similar in 
scale, block size, and configuration as traditional neighborhoods, 
simply at a higher residential intensity. Neighborhoods directly adja­
cent to downtown tend to be urban neighborhoods, which are more 
intense with housing due to their proximity to downtown jobs. 
Urban neighborhoods often follow a traditional block pattern 
similar to that found in Huning Highland, Silver Hill, and Univer­
sity Heights. The block and alley structure of these neighborhoods, 
and those directly surrounding downtown, can easily support more 
intense housing. Intensification of the neighborhoods surround­
ing downtown is unlikely, however, but the edges of downtown 
may develop similar characteristics as it transitions to surrounding 
neighborhoods.

INSTITUTIONAL STATIONS 	
[T | S]
The study area did not include any purely Institutional (I) stations. 
However there are many large institutional uses in Albuquerque 
which may be serviced by future transit.
Institutional Stations tend to be primarily destinations for either 
employment or activities. Commuter universities and large hospital 
campuses are often large enough to be single users of transit sta­
tions, which would fall into this category. While the University of 
New Mexico’s central campus falls into the next category, the North 
Campus and South Campus as well as CNM will likely fall into this 
category when bus rapid transit service is developed along Univer­
sity Boulevard. As destinations, Institutional Stations are subject to 
rush hours and periods of inactivity.
Because transit is used for commuting to and from institutions in 
these settings, pedestrian access and protections between transit 
stations and entries to institutions must be closely analyzed. Areas 
outside of the transit-entry linkages are likely to be automobile-
dominant. 
INSTITUTIONAL HYBRID STATIONS 	
[T]
Institutional Hybrid Stations (IH) combine aspects of Institutional 
Stations with Urban Center or Neighborhood Center Stations. They 
are typically located along a corridor where a large institution oc­
cupies one side of the street and the opposite side of the street is the 
edge of a neighborhood or urban center. Along the Central Avenue 
ART line, Cedar, University, and Cornell Stations are categorized as 
Institutional Hybrid Stations.
IH stations tend to be major employment destinations which can 
attract more intensive non-residential development adjacent to the 
station than a Neighborhood Center or Urban Center Station might 
attract. Depending upon the type of institution, they may have 
ridership spikes that differ from the typical employment rush hours, 
such as those caused by hospital shift changes.
Similar to Downtown Stations, IH stations experience higher than 
normal ridership and subsequently high pedestrian volumes. The 
streetscape details of focus in Downtown Stations apply to IH sta­
tions. However, the pedestrian movement patterns analyzed in an 
IH station area must also account for a higher volume of access to 
the institution, a single destination more significant than others, 
rather than a relatively even distribution of destinations in a down­
town.
Station area plans around IH stations must consider increases in 
bicycle and vehicular traffic volumes in addition to pedestrian 
volumes. While driving can often be significantly reduced in 
downtown, transit has a lesser effect in institutional environments. 
Increased vehicular traffic means that pedestrian and bicyclist safety 
requires more interventions than in other station areas.
Bicycle networks in IH station areas draw from a larger service area 
than other station area types because institutions have much larger 
trade areas than urban and neighborhood centers. The bicycle net­
work must be complete and adequately provisioned in all directions 
and often along multiple parallel paths. At the University of New 
Mexico, for instance, campus occupies three quarters of a mile along 
Central Avenue and has a strong relationship with a satellite campus 
approximately one mile south. Multiple parallel north-south bicycle 
routes are necessary in addition to east-west routes serving down­
town and the rest of the Central Avenue corridor. 
NEIGHBORHOOD CENTER STATIONS	
[T] 
Neighborhood Center (NC) stations are another uncommon station 
type in Albuquerque; Solano is the local example. These stations are 
located at the portion of a neighborhood that is its most intensive 
in terms of density and mix of uses. Along the ART trajectory 
analyzed, neighborhood centers are located at Central Avenue, 
where the ART stations are. As such, station locations and centers 
align well. Other neighborhoods, such as those north and south of 
downtown, may be served by future transit in locations outside of 
the neighborhood center.
Stations in neighborhood centers primarily serve residents in their 
daily commutes and as they access major designations outside of 
their neighborhood, such as downtown, institutions, or the fair­
grounds. Neighborhood centers may also act as destinations where 
they have established and active main streets. Solano is an example 
of a neighborhood center that acts as a destination for people from 
outside of the neighborhood. Those neighborhood centers that 
attract outsiders are likely to run into issues between residents and 
the intensity of development in the center as parking and develop­
ment cause friction. The best means of alleviating this friction is to 
establish more neighborhood centers, connected by transit. 
NEIGHBORHOOD STATIONS 	
[T]
Neighborhood (N) stations were not studied in this process, howev­
er the type may be established in the future in Albuquerque. These 
stations serve neighborhoods but are not located in the neighbor­
hood center. Often this occurs where major corridors run along the 
edge of a neighborhood, and those corridors have either not been 
zoned commercial or they have but they do not support commer­
cial activity. Neighborhood stations do not serve as destinations for 
those outside of the neighborhood. Rather they are used primarily 
for commuting. These station types are not ideal unless they are sur­
rounded by very intense residential development.

SUBURBAN CENTER STATIONS 	
[S]
Suburban Center (SC) stations are generally uncommon, occurring 
where neighborhood structure contradicts the character of adjacent 
major corridors. These areas are close-in, meaning they are relatively 
near the city center such that the neighborhood fabric is made of 
gridded and interconnected streets. However they are suburban 
due to the character of development and zoning along adjacent 
corridors. Interestingly, this is a common type in Albuquerque. 
Yucca, Atrisco, Washington, San Mateo and Louisiana fall into this 
category; they have relatively traditional neighborhood fabric while 
the adjacent corridor has been developed with strip commercial and 
other heavily automobile-centric patterns.
These station areas are in need of significant redevelopment, but 
due to the scale of property ownership, they are not likely to move 
to the Transitional Station category. Transitional areas have the abil­
ity to be redeveloped on a large scale because property ownership 
along the corridor tends to be in large sites. The close-in Suburban 
Center Station then aims to become a neighborhood center but 
could become a larger and more intense town center if development 
is coordinated between many property owners.
EXURBAN CENTER STATIONS 	
[S]
Exurban Center (EC) stations present the greatest potential for 
return on investment from ART, according to the study by Center 
for Neighborhood Technology. These areas represent such potential 
because they tend to be very underdeveloped and have property 
ownership patterns that are very large under single ownership. 
The stations in the category in Albuquerque include Unser, Coors, 
Wyoming, Eubank, Juan Tabo and Tramway. These often include 
suburban shopping centers or warehouse areas. Shopping centers 
are the most common condition near these stations, a land use that 
is subject to regular redevelopment, typically on a 20 year cycle. 
Suburban shopping centers are often built inexpensively and the 
majority of the land area is given to surface parking lots. Recent 
trends in shopping center redevelopment have been to establish 
more intensive centers with retail and housing, which act as town 
centers for surrounding suburban areas.
Suburban and Exurban Town Center Station areas tend to lack a 
sense of place, following common patterns of single-use, segregated 
development. Stations in these areas can reduce overall traffic in 
two ways: capturing ridership for the transit system and capturing 
trips from the surrounding suburban residential areas by providing a 
number of destinations in a single location.
These stations act equally as destinations and sources of ridership. 
Ridership is built both by residents within the town center redevel­
opment and as mini park-and-rides providing a means of shifting 
from cars to transit for surrounding residential areas. Because these 
town center redevelopments tend to be large and service a very large 
surrounding suburban area, they attract and retain national retail 
tenants and grocers. Because these types of tenants are not located 
near the city center, suburban and exurban town center stations are 
destinations for residents of closer-in neighborhoods. 
SUBURBAN COMMUTER STATIONS 	
[S]
Suburban Commuter (SC) stations were not studied in this process, 
however the type is included further out on the ART system. These 
stations are purely utility, aimed at capturing drivers and moving 
them onto the transit system. This station type creates very little 
value and should generally be avoided. Suburban commuter stations 
are appropriate in suburban areas far outside of the city center, 
where the corridor they are located along is zoned purely residential 
with little likelihood for redevelopment. Instead of Suburban Com­
muter Stations, stations should be located instead where they are 
able to generate redevelopment and infill, creating value. g

RETAIL
Urban retail shares similarities with subur­
ban retail, but has unique needs. Walkabil­
ity is a single issue that can determine the 
success or failure of urban retail, according 
to Robert Gibbs, author of The Principals 
of Urban Retail. The Gibbs analysis of Nob 
Hill concluded that the biggest threat to 
successful retail in Lower Nob Hill was the 
speed of traffic on Central. 
Another important asset for successful 
urban retail is on street parking. With­
out the convenience of large parking lots 
enjoyed by suburban retail, main streets 
must have reliable spaces on the street as 
well as larger facilities nearby. While parking 
exists along Central, it is not consistent, and 
high demand areas link University and Nob 
Hill suffer from a lack of available facilities 
within a five minute walk.
To assure successful retail in the TOD 
environment, the following barriers must be 
addressed throughout the corridor. Many 
of the issues addressed in First-Last Mile 
related to walkability, are also important to 
the success of urban retail.

 B.01
COMMERCIAL CORRIDOR ZON­
ING CAUSED THE PROLIFERA­
TION OF LOW VALUE RETAIL 
AND AUTOMOBILE-CENTRIC SITE 
PLANNING.
All of Central Avenue is zoned for commer­
cial, as are other major corridors throughout 
Albuquerque. This practice was common 
across the country with the advent of eu­
clidean zoning, based on uses instead of on 
the form of the built environment. The over 
abundance of available commercial land 
eliminated the land scarcity that encour­
ages more intense development along main 
streets. With miles and miles of commercial, 
low intensity commercial users proliferated. 
The resulting spread-out commercial corri­
dors required people to drive between busi­
nesses, and for those businesses to provide 
parking. The majority of Central Avenue is 
the result of this process: low intensity com­
mercial uses with a lot of parking.
Research has shown that retail streets only 
function successfully for short distances. 
Best practices today recommend retail 
streets be no longer than ¼ mile, up to ½ 
mile in rare cases. This is indirectly cor­
roborated by the highly-studied design of 
malls, which are typically based upon a ¼ 
mile length. Historic main streets follow a 
similar rule. In order to produce successful 
retail along Central Avenue, the portions of 
the corridor with commercial uses must be 
significantly reduced.

CAR SHARE
Car sharing programs allow people to have 
on-demand access to a shared fleet of ve­
hicles on an as-needed basis. Car sharing is 
similar to conventional car rental programs 
with a few key differences:
+
+
System users must be members of a car 
sharing organization.
+
+
Fee structures typically emphasize 
short-term rentals rather than daily or 
weekly rentals.
+
+
Vehicle reservations and access is “self-
service.”
+
+
Vehicle locations are widely distributed 
rather than concentrated.
Car share programs reduce the need for 
businesses or households to own vehicles, 
and reduce personal transportation costs 
and vehicle miles traveled (VMT). Through 
car share, individuals gain access to vehicles 
by joining an organization that maintains a 
fleet of cars and light trucks in a network of 
locations.
Car sharing has sometimes been referred 
to as the “missing link” in the package of 
alternatives to the private automobile. For 
example, vehicles available near a person’s 
workplace or school can enable them to 
commute to work via transit or other 
means, knowing that they will have a car 
share vehicle available during the day only 
if needed for work or personal trips. It has 
proven successful in reducing both house­
hold vehicle ownership and the percentage 
of employees who drive alone because of the 
need to have a car for errands during the 
workday. As a result, car sharing can be an 
important tool to reduce parking demand.

to car share programs in the City in several 
ways. These include:
+
+
Develop direct partnerships, where 
parking spaces are provided to or leased 
to car share programs. Such arrange­
ments would ideally be made in higher 
density areas.
+
+
Encourage partnerships between 
developers and car share companies to 
ensure new developments, particularly 
those along the ART corridor, have 
such services available to tenants. In 
addition, car share can be used as a 
strategy to reduce the parking require­
ments asked of developers.

TWO-WAY CONVERSION
Coal Avenue and Lead Avenue are currently 
a one-way couplet ranging from Downtown 
through Nob Hill. One-way couplets are 
used in urban areas to move high volumes of 
traffic to and from activity cores. However, 
couplets are not typically abutting residen­
tial communities, as this couplet does in 
Albuquerque. These couplets introduce high 
speeds and traffic volumes to residential 
communities, which have resulted in signifi­
cant safety concerns from the community 
due to frequent collisions that affect homes 
and residents.
It is recommended that the couplet be 
converted to two two-way streets. While 
the costs of signalization, reduced turning 
movements, and potential for capacity loss 
are potential drawbacks, the benefits would 
be significant for the residential commu­
nities currently along the Coal and Lead 
corridors. Benefits would include traffic 
calming impacts, improved safety, increased 
connectivity and access, and a potential 
decrease in VMT as a result of more direct 
connectivity.
 A.15 
Implement the Lead & Coal two-way con­
version that has been recommended through 
multiple planning documents over the last 
decade.

It will be important over time to tailor the 
parking policies to each station area so that 
parking availability and operation is clear 
for visitors, while its potential impact on 
neighborhoods are mitigated. The program 
elements for each station area are likely to 
be slightly different, but the sections below 
provide a summary of some approaches that 
should be a part of the thinking that goes 
into detailed action plans for each station 
area. These action plans should be based on 
real data (supply and use of parking, square 
footage of development) and include fo­
cused outreach with the affected businesses 
and neighborhoods. See individual station 
area plans for recommended strategies.
PARKING DEMAND
Achieving a balance between parking supply 
and development is a critical challenge in 
developing transit-oriented development 
(TOD). Denser mixed-use development 
sited around high quality transit increases 
the potential for multiple trips to be linked 
through alternative and active modes and 
fosters the type of urban form that creates 
a “park once” environment for motorists. 
In addition, TOD promotes self-selection, 
improving transit access for households 
who wish to be car free, or a one car family. 
Recent research of TOD projects around the 
United States suggests that TOD residents 
are twice as likely not to own a car as other 
households, while they are two to five times 
more likely to commute by transit than oth­
ers in their region. 
A recent study produced by Smart Growth 
America found that despite building less 
parking than recommended by the Institute 
of Transportation Engineers’ (ITE) Trip 
Generation and Parking Generation guides, 
TODs across the country were producing 
a ratio of parking demand between 58 and 
84 percent of actual supply provided. One 
reason for this is that TODs produce far 
fewer vehicle trips than standard trip genera­
tion models estimate. Furthermore, TODs 
produce lower rates of parking demand than 
what is estimated by traditional parking 
generation ratios because many sites share 
parking between commercial and residential 
uses, unbundle parking from residential 
rents, and charge market rates for commer­
cial parking. Despite reduced levels of de­
mand compared to traditional development, 
most TODs with residential and commer­
cial components require some amount of 
parking to achieve market feasibility and to 
secure project financing. 
The following strategies offer solutions to 
best manage existing parking supply and ac­
commodate growth and parking demand at 
TOD zones along the ART corridor.

 B.16
ADJUST TIME LIMITS
It is recommended that on-street metered 
parking throughout the corridor shift to 
demand-based pricing to achieve parking 
availability goals rather than time limits 
to achieve a vague turnover goal. While 
relaxing time limit restrictions makes park­
ing more convenient for drivers, doing so 
does not mean that all people will park 
longer. Extending time limits simply allows 
individuals to park longer if they want to. 
At present, specific zones, such as Nob Hill 
have a multitude of on-street parking time 
limits within a couple of blocks.
It recommended that various time limits be 
consolidated to fewer options, to improve 
the ease of understanding options. In addi­
tion, limits less than 2-hours may be detri­
mental to districts looking to achieve a “park 
once” environment where patrons can park 
once and frequent shops, dining, and enter­
tainment all within a single trip. Moving to 
a demand-based pricing system reduces the 
reliance on time limits. Fewer time limits 
result in fewer citations and a more positive 
parking experience for visitors. 
 A.16 
Potential time limit adjustments strategies 
to metered parking along the ART corridor 
include the following:
+
+
Three/Four hours: The typical/de­
fault parking meter time limit could 
be relaxed to three to four hours. This 
allows the City to try relaxed time 
limits, without eliminating them. Four 
hours typically allows plenty of time 
for someone to dine and run errands 
without fearing a parking citation. 
+
+
No limit (with demand-based pric­
ing): Demand-based pricing eliminates 
the need for parking meter time limits, 
as availability is managed through price 
rather than arbitrary limits to how long 
one can stay. This is a significant change 
for many cities, most of whom have 
historically relied on short time limits 
and a focus on turnover. 
+
+
Extend Hours of Operation: Hours 
of operation should be matched to the 
need for open parking spaces based on 
business hours and occupancy trends. 
Current time restrictions are only until 
6 p.m. and lose their ability to create 
turnover thereafter. For a district like 
Nob Hill where there is significant 
parking demand generated by bars 
and restaurants hours of enforcement 
should be extended to 8 p.m.

DEMAND-BASED PRICING
Progressive parking policies manage a 
city’s existing supply of parking. A key 
component of any such policy is demand-
based pricing (also referred to as demand-
responsive pricing, variable-rate pricing, 
performance-based pricing, or dynamic 
pricing). Demand-based pricing charges the 
lowest possible rate that achieves availability 
targets, better aligning price and demand 
to ensure there is always an open parking 
space. 
Researchers have determined the ideal 
on-street parking occupancy rate to be 
around 85%, which leaves roughly one to 
two spaces available per block so that cars 
no longer have to circle the block to find 
parking. To achieve this, cities increase the 
cost of parking or reduce time limits in areas 
with higher occupancy rates, and decrease 
the cost of park­
ing or increase 
time limits in 
areas with occu­
pancy rates lower 
than 85%. The 
optimal occupancy rate for off-street facili­
ties is 90-95%.
Drivers are either bargain hunters or con­
venience seekers. Convenience seekers are 
more willing to pay for an available front 
door spot. Many shoppers and diners are 
typically less sensitive to parking charges 
because they stay for shorter periods of time 
than an employee or all-day visitors. Many 
longer-stay parkers find it worthwhile to 
walk a block to save on parking fees. With 
proper pricing, the bargain hunters will 
choose currently underutilized lots and 
more distant meters, leaving the prime 
spots free for those convenience seekers. For 
merchants, it is important to make prime 
spots available for these people: those who 
are willing to pay a fee to park are also those 
who are willing to spend money in stores 
and restaurants.

RESIDENTIAL PARKING PERMIT 
PROGRAM
A Residential Parking Permit (RPP) 
program operates by exempting permit­
ted vehicles from the parking restrictions 
and time limits for non-metered, on-street 
parking spaces within a geographic area. The 
primary goal is to manage parking spillover 
into residential neighborhoods. RPPs work 
best in neighborhoods that are impacted by 
high parking demand from other uses, such 
as:
+
+
Universities, colleges, neighborhood 
schools, or hospitals
+
+
Transit stations
+
+
Popular commercial, retail, entertain­
ment, tourist, or recreational destina­
tions
By managing spillover, RPPs can ensure 
that residential neighborhoods are not 
overwhelmed by commuters, employees, 
or visitors, thereby enabling local residents 
to park their vehicles on-street. Most RPP 
programs allow those without a permit to 
park for generally two to four hours during 
a specified period, such as 8:00 AM to 6:00 
PM, Monday to Friday. Permit holders are 
exempt from these regulations. Ownership 
of a permit, however, does not guarantee the 
availability of a parking space.
Throughout Albuquerque there are ap­
proximately 80 blocks spread amongst 30 
streets with existing RPP zones within three 
blocks of the ART corridor. The City allows 
for the establishment of an RPP zone if a 
study determines that a significant portion 
of on-street parking is being utilized by non-
residents of a residential street. The existing 
regulations allow for each address within 
an RPP zone to obtain up to three permits, 
and a permit per unit for lots or addresses 
with multiple units. Permits are issued to 
residents free of charge. Unlike RPP Zones 
in most cities, some RPP Zones along the 
ART corridor, such as the U Zone, are in ef­
fect during the daytime in order to mitigate 
concerns of spillover parking from UNM. 
This leads to many of the spaces remaining 
empty or underutilized throughout the day 
when residents are at work despite the pres­
ence of nearby shops and restaurants that 
are open during that time.

SHARED PARKING
S
hared parking agreements 
are arrangements with pri­
vate parking lot owners that 
provide for privately owned 
off-street parking to be available to 
the public during specified periods 
of time, usually when the parking 
lot is in low demand for its associ­
ated tenants.
A typical example of shared parking would 
be a land use that creates parking demand 
during the day, which could then become 
available to the public during non-business 
hours or at other times when there is an 
overabundance of available parking. The 
agreement with the parking lot owner 
would stipulate the times during which 
public users may park in the lot and terms 
for compensation and operation. 
 A.22 
The City could enter into three types of 
agreements with a private property owner. 
These are:
1.	 Leasing of a private lot: Under this 
arrangement, parking spaces would es­
sentially be “rented” from the property 
owner and the City would be entitled 
to establish regulations during “shared” 
use hours. Upgrades (lighting, striping, 
signage, etc.) could be made and the 
City would enforce compliance with 
regulations.
2.	 Private ownership, public enforce­
ment: Under this arrangement the 
private property owner would open 
their lot to the public and establish 
regulations (including any pricing). The 
owner could choose to charge for park­
ing, depending on parking demand. 
The City would enforce compliance 
with regulations and collect citation 
revenue. 
3.	 Third-party management: It may be 
simpler and more cost-effective for the 
City to contract with a private com­
pany with experience facilitating shared 
parking arrangements instead of creat­
ing and managing its own agreements. 
This company would also establish 
regulations (including any pricing).

 B.23
VALET PARKING
Municipal valet services may be used to 
manage spikes in parking demand in busy 
commercial districts. These services offer an 
easy alternative to finding on-street parking 
by allowing drivers to drop-off their car at 
one location, shop, dine, and run errands, 
and then pick up their car when they are 
done. Unlike other valets that serve only 
one business, municipal valets are open to 
the public. With just a few on-street spots 
at each valet location, municipalities can 
maximize their existing parking supply by 
having the valet use underutilized parking 
lots and garages.
Valet parking provides an opportunity to 
shift demand to off-street lots and increase 
the ease of parking for visitors to high-de­
mand areas. It can also increase the effective 
parking supply through more efficient use, 
as valet operators can tandem or triple park 
vehicles. Valet parking is useful in evenings 
and during seasonal or event peaks, particu­
larly to support restaurants and the theater. 
Typically, these programs can be funded 
through neighborhood business associations 
or PBDs. In general, cities or businesses 
enter into a contract with one or more valet 
operators to provide the service. In order 
to make valet services a single, seamless 
operation, consistent branding (signage and 
uniform) should be required.
 A.23 
Pilot municipal valet services in strategic 
neighborhoods like Downtown and Nob 
Hill. Selection of valet parking drop-off 
locations need to consider how the loss of 
on-street parking spaces will affect parking 
behavior and whether drop-off and pick-
up would negatively affect traffic flow and 
street operations. These locations could be 
coordinated with existing passenger loading 
zones to minimize parking loss.
