EDO SUMMARY
EDo is the eastern extension of downtown 
and the hub of innovation as well as the 
commercial heart of two historic neighbor­
hoods. The area north of Central Avenue 
and South of Lomas Boulevard will see a 
significant transformation as a combination 
of light industrial makerspace shops pop up 
around the rail line, anchored by the larger, 
more intense uses around Innovate ABQ. 
The east end of EDo is primarily residential 
with the exception of some 1-3 story retail 
shops fronting Central Avenue. The neigh­
borhood has a stable urban character with a 
good mix of uses and housing types.
EDO
STATION AREA
The EDo station is east of Innovate ABQ 
at Walter, and will serve as a neighborhood 
center for the area. The station is centrally 
located and will fully serve the users travel­

DESTINATIONS
The section of Central in EDo is not con­
sistently a main street at present, but it con­
tains a number of destination restaurants, 
frequented by both nearby residents and

tracks. Being directly adjacent to the tracks, 
Innovate ABQ will provide activity for both 
downtown and EDo.

PARKING ASSESSMENT
There are over 1,000 on-street parking 
spaces in EDo and Huning Highlands not 
including Central and Broadway. Under 
current City rules, property owners in Hun­
ing Highlands, not living on Central Av­
enue or Broadway Boulevard, may request 
a neighborhood parking permit program 
for their street or area. Residential parking 
permits protect on-street spaces for residents 
only from 8:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m.1 
Parking concerns in EDo related to TOD 
will likely be related to a perceived loss 
or scarcity of parking rather than actual 
rates of use. To receive the greatest return 
on investment from ART, it is critical that 
strategies focus on efficiently distributing 
parking demand to underutilized facilities, 
leveraging opportunities for shared parking 
partnerships, and integration of parking into 
districtwide mobility branding and com­
munications.

NEIGHBORHOODS
The EDo station area includes two neigh­
borhoods: MARTINEZTOWN, north of 
Lomas, and HUNING HIGHLAND, from 
MLK to south of Iron. Central Avenue is a 
main street that serves the neighborhoods. 
HUNING HIGHLAND is known for its 
significantly intact historic district, and it 
has a Historic Protection Overlay (HPO) 
in the draft IDO. MARTINEZTOWN 
was established in the 1800's with a mix of 
formal and informal streets, conforming to 
the topography north of MLK Ave.
Both neighborhoods are located between 
two major transportation corridors: I-25 
to the east and the train tracks and depot 
to the west. Given these barriers, HUN­
ING HIGHLAND has a very orthogonal  
neighborhood structure. MARTINEZ­
TOWN has a more organic structure due 
to the street trajectories and a few large 
non-residential uses along I-25: the hospital 
district, Embassy Suites, and Albuquerque 
High School.

 B.02
Street trees are inconsistent in 
Huning Highland and essentially 
non-existent in Martineztown.
With very few exceptions, EDo’s streets are 
devoid of street trees, especially in Marti­
neztown. Street trees play a vital role to the 
walkability and livability of districts and 
neighborhoods. Physically, they provide 
shade, in an environment where shade is 
important. Psychologically, trees play a vital 
role in human wellness, and they add eco­
nomic value to adjacent properties.
The arid climate and lack of water is a bar­
rier to providing street trees. However there 
are trees more well adapted to the climate 
that work well as street trees. Without 
street trees, sun on streets and sidewalks 
has a heat-island effect, creating a high-heat 
microclimate.

 B.03
Travel lanes on major thru-streets 
are too wide, causing high speeds 
and deteriorating pedestrian and 
cyclist safety.
Because of the street network limitations, 
the thru-streets are over-burdened with 
vehicular traffic needs. To accommodate 
those needs, the streets have been redesigned 
over time to meet suburban, high-speed 
arterial standards. This includes travel lanes 
up to 12 feet wide, broad gutter pans, large 
curb return radii, and dedicated turn lanes. 
These accommodations are provided at the 
expense of pedestrians and cyclists. 
Pedestrians are provided sidewalks often too 
narrow for comfort, and adjacent to high-
speed vehicular traffic. Road widening has 
removed street trees and lighting has been 
scaled to high-speed traffic. Large curb re­
turn radii threaten pedestrians in crosswalks. 
Cyclists are similarly affected overall by 
bike lanes directly adjacent to travel lanes. 
Recently the bike lanes on Lead, Coal, and 
MLK have been provided with buffers from 
travel lanes to increase safety.
