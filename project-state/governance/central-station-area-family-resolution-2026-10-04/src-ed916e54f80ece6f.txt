UNIVERSITY
SUMMARY
The University District has a long and var­
ied history, common to residential districts 
adjacent to Universities and large employers. 
The area employers have been automobile 
oriented for much of this time, consolidated 
within inward-facing campuses. The neigh­
borhood has fared relatively well through 
the influx of transient student populations, 
but many of its current issues are related: 
lack of sufficient property maintenance, 
parking in the wrong locations, illegal 
housing unit conversions, open spaces and 
parking areas unsupervised at night. The 
neighborhood itself, split into more than 
one distinct entity, has advocated for many 
reasonable changes over decades, having 
seen few of those changes happen. The 
area also suffers from large land users - the 
employers, schools, and grave yard - which 
concentrate traffic on to a limited number 
of north-south streets. With many students 
and residents in the area, safe walking and 
cycling routes are needed.
ART can mark the beginning of a new 
chapter. Along with this transit investment, 
UNM is also focused on a new identity. As 
they move towards a primarily residential 
school, rather than a commuter school, 
neighborhood dynamics may shift. In their 
new image, UNM can refocus on the edge 
of their campus and its interface with the 
City. Their investment in the new Physical 
and Astronomy Interdisciplinary Science 
Building at Yale Park marks such a shift. The 
resurgent commercial core at Harvard Street 
can be bolstered and grow into a small dis­
trict that supports both the University and 
the neighborhood. Redevelopment along 
Central Avenue can be focused on increas­
ing the residential population and address­
ing long standing parking issues. Neighbor­
hood beautification efforts can be addressed, 
helping increase transit ridership.

NEIGHBORHOODS
The station area south of UNM includes 
two neighborhoods: University Heights and 
Silver Hill. Both neighborhoods have active 
associations and have advocated for very rea­
sonable changes since at least 1978. While 
a few of those changes have taken place, 
many have not and will re-appear later in 
this section.
UNIVERSITY HEIGHTS is located 
south of Central Avenue and east of Yale 
Boulevard. The neighborhood is typified by 
a varied mix of housing types and density, 
a pattern historic to the district. For a time, 
the neighborhood had significant issues 
with student housing intrusion, which has 
receded in recent years. While higher inten­
sity development has been permitted in the 
neighborhood historically, recent experienc­
es include illegal unit additions to existing 
buildings. These illegal additions are often 
parked within former front yards, contribut­
ing to a lack of public realm safety. Similarly 
as a largely rental neighborhood, there is 
little maintenance of park strips, which have 
become treeless and bare in most instances, 
reducing walkability.
SILVER HILL is located south of Central 
Avenue and west of Yale Boulevard. The 
neighborhood is well known for its historic 
district, the pinnacle of which is the sec­
tion of Silver Avenue with a median. The 
neighborhood is comprised of primarily 
single family houses with some multi-family 
buildings, both historic and new. Student 
housing has been less prevalent in Silver Hill 
than University Heights. University Boule­
vard currently splits the neighborhood into 
two sections, because of its high speeds and 
six-travel-lane section. There is some historic 
retail in Silver Hill along Central Avenue, 
but much of this has deteriorated over time.
SPRUCE PARK is north of Central and 
west of University, but was outside of the 
planning area for this process. It is a lower 
density, largely single-family detached 
neighborhood with some multi-family and 
mixed-use along MLK Jr. Avenue.

YALE BOULEVARD 
STREETSCAPE
The existing condition of Yale Bou­
levard is automobile oriented and does 
not support active modes of transporta­
tion. With the potential of new mixed-use 
development, and in proximity to a large 
pedestrian population, the street design 
should be changed.
Currently the street's pedestrian realm 
has 8-foot sidewalks, and a wide planting 
strip. South of Silver this design should be 
retained, with the addition of pedestrian-
scaled lighting. From Silver to Central, the 
design should be adjusted to be more urban, 
with trees in long tree wells and a mostly 
paved streetscape, supportive of ground 
floor retail.
The existing vehicular space is a 3-lane 
section with 12-foot travel lanes, which 
encourages high speeds. From Silver south 
to Coal, this section should be changed to 
accommodate on street parking and slower 
vehicle movements. With simple striping, 
the road can be changed to include two 10-
foot travel lanes and two 7 to 8-foot parking 
lanes. Restriping would help provide addi­
tional parking as well as calm traffic through 
this pedestrian-oriented district.

BICYCLE BOULEVARD
Buena Vista Drive provides a direct

need to be coordinated along the street's

 B.02
LANDSCAPING WITHIN PARK 
STRIPS IS INCONSISTENT WITHIN 
THE STATION AREA.
Most streets lack street trees and are incon­
sistent in their landscaping. While the local 
climate can be difficult for maintaining 
street trees, their benefits outweigh the chal­
lenges. Historically the neighborhood streets 
were lined with trees, Silver Avenue being 
a example, which was recently replanted. 
Street trees increase walkability, reduce 
urban heat islands, and provide signifi­
cant economic, psychological and physical 
benefits.
The neighborhood has been discussing the 
benefit of street trees for a long time. The 
1985 Sector Plan for Silver Hill discussed 
the prospect of a neighborhood nursery to 
develop a stock of street trees.
The need for street trees is a city-wide issue, 
clearly difficult to solve on a neighborhood 
basis. Given recent interest in health as a 
result of the built environment, it should 
be possible to form a non-profit similar 
to Casey Trees, in Washington DC. Since 
2002, Casey Trees has been planting street 
trees, maintaining existing street trees, and 
training residents in tree care. A similar pro­
gram could be locally established to address 
the street tree problem.

 B.03
THE AREAS NEAREST TO 
CENTRAL AVENUE HAVE A 
PUBLIC SAFETY PROBLEM.
Crime has been a concern in the area for 
quite some time. The 1978 and 1985 Sector 
Plans discussed concerns along Central Ave 
and in relation to the increasing student 
population living in University Heights at 
the time. Of particular concern was Yale 
Park, located on the UNM campus, where 
drug use was cited as a top issue. In recent 
years crime and the perception of crime has 
been decreasing, as it has nationally. How­
ever the perception still exists and stigma­
tizes the area.
The built environment plays a role in crime 
prevention. Principles of Crime Prevention 
Through Environmental Design (CPTED) 
provide strategies to reduce crime by altering 
the built environment. One safety issue is 
parking between the sidewalk and buildings 
in University Heights. This has occurred 
over time as buildings have been converted, 
often illegally, to multi-family. Parking 
between the street and building across the 
frontage violates CPTED principles of ter­
ritoriality and eyes on the street.
