POTENTIAL AMENDMENT TO ROUTE 66 ACTION PLAN
The City of Albuquerque is in the midst 
of implementing an 18-month federal 
planning grant, The Corridors Initiative, to 
develop strategies for leveraging investments 
in new transit opportunities and in new 
growth regulations along Albuquerque's 
Central Avenue Corridor.
From the outset, intentions were to con­
solidate and build on all the work that has 
come before, then test ideas for guiding 
future growth with an intensive, week long 
public workshop. Held in March of 2017, 
the workshop was a collaboration between 
citizens, community leaders and a team of 
TOD consultants to explore opportunities 
ahead for those who live, work and shop 
along the region’s most vital corridor.
Throughout the workshop week, the collab­
oration took the form of special topic meet­
ings, work-in-progress reviews, exercises to 
test alternative strategies for strengthening 
capacities of communities, and for making 
the most of what could potentially amount 
to $2 billion of new investment.
Going into the workshop, some 1,000 
people had already taken part in various 
meetings to set the table for a more focused 
discussion. Coming out of that week of idea 
testing are recommendations embodied in 
this report for specific strategies that can be 
used anywhere along the Central Avenue 
Corridor to connect surrounding neighbor­
hoods to transit, to upgrade pedestrian and 
cyclist amenities and to nurture community 
and reinforce a sense of place.

ALBUQUERQUE, NM
OPPORTUNITY
ZONING + STREET INVESTMENT
Albuquerque is experiencing two significant 
changes in the way it guides future growth: 
a revision of the citywide zoning code and 
a major transportation investment along 
Central Avenue. This is a time to inspire – 
and support – dramatic, positive change for 
the Main Street of the greater Albuquerque 
Metro. 
SUPPORT
FEDERAL TRANSIT 
ADMINISTRATION GRANT
The Federal Transit Administration awarded 
the City of Albuquerque’s Transit, Planning 
and Economic Development departments 
a $860,000 TOD planning grant. Since 
mid 2016, community members along the 
Central Avenue Corridor participated in 
workshops and in-person and online ex­
changes about moving Central Avenue from 
“good to great.” Together, they helped shape 
a 2017 Draft Work Plan aimed toward a 
Central Avenue that connects people, cre­
ates great places and drives opportunity.
WORKSHOP WEEK
TASK FOR PUBLIC ENGAGEMENT 
AND REPORT
For the March workshop week, a team made 
up of local and national planners, architects, 
economic analysts and engineers served 
as resources to community stakeholders. 
Making use of all the data and community 
conversations that came before, participants 
explored ideas and options for accelerating 
opportunities for families and businesses 
and for building on the existing quality of 
life along the 17-mile stretch of Central 
Avenue from Unser Boulevard on the west 
to Tramway Boulevard on the east.
GOALS
In the 12,000-acre Central Avenue Corridor 
(old Route 66), the goals are to develop a 
range of strategies to:
+
+
Better align the ambitions of individual neighborhoods and 
businesses along the corridor with opportunities that improved 
transit is likely to inspire.
+
+
Position plans for new development to maximize the potential 
for an estimated $2 billion of real estate (re)investment.
+
+
Assure savings (an estimated $1 billion) from poverty reduc­
tion, improved transit access and other efficiencies that benefit 
everyone in Albuquerque.
+
+
Maximize the potential for job creation (an estimated potential 
of more than 9,500 jobs) along the corridor.

ALBUQUERQUE, NM
DOWNTOWN STATIONS	
[U]
Downtown (DT) stations are located within the urban core of 
downtown Albuquerque. Downtown ART service runs on parallel 
roads off of Central due to a narrow right-of-way. This loop includes 
more frequent stops than in other portions of the transit system due 
to the high concentration of destinations and future high concentra­
tion of housing and overall ridership. 
In many dense, historic downtowns, station location has relatively 
little effect on the surrounding blocks. However, in Albuquerque, 
downtown stations will permit increased development on adjacent 
blocks by significantly reducing parking demand in the core. The 
reduced parking demand is due to workers commuting in on transit 
as well as downtown residents commuting out on transit. 
While parking is not required for downtown uses, leasing office 
space, retail space, and renting housing without parking can be 
difficult in an automobile-oriented city. As transit service increases, 
however, the demand for parking should decrease. Without every­
thing needed for daily life within downtown at present, the land 
near transit stations is likely to be more valuable as a result, connect­
ing to services and housing outside of the district.
Increased development and activity will significantly increase pedes­
trian traffic throughout the downtown. While pedestrian consider­
ations are extremely important in all station areas, they are critical 
in downtown station areas. Considerations must be made for safe, 
comfortable, and convenient pedestrian movements, including:
+
+
Crosswalk design and frequency
+
+
Provide leading pedestrian intervals (LPIs)
+
+
Pedestrian crosswalk signal initiation
+
+
Provision for parallel parking
+
+
Vehicle speeds
+
+
Sidewalk width
+
+
Sidewalk condition
+
+
Sidewalk shade
+
+
Sidewalk lighting
+
+
Building frontage design
+
+
Frequency of building entrances
+
+
Shielding of parking
To have a successful downtown, vehicular accommodations need 
to be reduced. Some degree of traffic congestion is to be expected 
in a downtown. Attempting to achieve a level of service better than 
F causes harm in intensive urban areas. In fact, many places are 
beginning to move away from level of service entirely because it is 
incompatible with walkable areas.

URBAN CENTER STATIONS	
[U]
Urban Center (UC) stations are located in or near high-intensity 
mixed-use areas, often including cultural institutions. Urban Cen­
ters are less intense than downtowns but significantly more intense 
than neighborhood centers. Rio Grande, West Downtown, and 
Bryn Mawr, are Urban Centers, including a mix of commercial, resi­
dential, and institutional uses within close proximity.
Urban Centers are destinations, typically drawing people from other 
parts of the city. As such, they can drive transit ridership, especially 
over weekends. Many urban centers have the capacity for increased 
housing and should provide sufficient residential density to meet 
their potential.
Like Downtown Stations, walkable streets are key to the success of 
urban centers. Due to typically high densities within urban centers, 
they tend to provide most needed services within the center area 
and are most efficiently traversed on foot or by bicycle. If an urban 
center is primarily serviced by automobiles, it is likely not supplying 
sufficient housing or is missing key services like a grocery. Excep­
tions to this rule may occur with major institutions with regional 
draws, like museums.
URBAN NEIGHBORHOOD STATIONS	
[U]
Albuquerque currently lacks many urban neighborhoods but they 
may be aspirational in the future. At this time, Walter is the only 
Urban Neighborhood. Urban neighborhoods (UN) are similar in 
scale, block size, and configuration as traditional neighborhoods, 
simply at a higher residential intensity. Neighborhoods directly adja­
cent to downtown tend to be urban neighborhoods, which are more 
intense with housing due to their proximity to downtown jobs. 
Urban neighborhoods often follow a traditional block pattern 
similar to that found in Huning Highland, Silver Hill, and Univer­
sity Heights. The block and alley structure of these neighborhoods, 
and those directly surrounding downtown, can easily support more 
intense housing. Intensification of the neighborhoods surround­
ing downtown is unlikely, however, but the edges of downtown 
may develop similar characteristics as it transitions to surrounding 
neighborhoods.

INSTITUTIONAL STATIONS 	
[T | S]
The study area did not include any purely Institutional (I) stations. 
However there are many large institutional uses in Albuquerque 
which may be serviced by future transit.
Institutional Stations tend to be primarily destinations for either 
employment or activities. Commuter universities and large hospital 
campuses are often large enough to be single users of transit sta­
tions, which would fall into this category. While the University of 
New Mexico’s central campus falls into the next category, the North 
Campus and South Campus as well as CNM will likely fall into this 
category when bus rapid transit service is developed along Univer­
sity Boulevard. As destinations, Institutional Stations are subject to 
rush hours and periods of inactivity.
Because transit is used for commuting to and from institutions in 
these settings, pedestrian access and protections between transit 
stations and entries to institutions must be closely analyzed. Areas 
outside of the transit-entry linkages are likely to be automobile-
dominant. 
INSTITUTIONAL HYBRID STATIONS 	
[T]
Institutional Hybrid Stations (IH) combine aspects of Institutional 
Stations with Urban Center or Neighborhood Center Stations. They 
are typically located along a corridor where a large institution oc­
cupies one side of the street and the opposite side of the street is the 
edge of a neighborhood or urban center. Along the Central Avenue 
ART line, Cedar, University, and Cornell Stations are categorized as 
Institutional Hybrid Stations.
IH stations tend to be major employment destinations which can 
attract more intensive non-residential development adjacent to the 
station than a Neighborhood Center or Urban Center Station might 
attract. Depending upon the type of institution, they may have 
ridership spikes that differ from the typical employment rush hours, 
such as those caused by hospital shift changes.
Similar to Downtown Stations, IH stations experience higher than 
normal ridership and subsequently high pedestrian volumes. The 
streetscape details of focus in Downtown Stations apply to IH sta­
tions. However, the pedestrian movement patterns analyzed in an 
IH station area must also account for a higher volume of access to 
the institution, a single destination more significant than others, 
rather than a relatively even distribution of destinations in a down­
town.
Station area plans around IH stations must consider increases in 
bicycle and vehicular traffic volumes in addition to pedestrian 
volumes. While driving can often be significantly reduced in 
downtown, transit has a lesser effect in institutional environments. 
Increased vehicular traffic means that pedestrian and bicyclist safety 
requires more interventions than in other station areas.
Bicycle networks in IH station areas draw from a larger service area 
than other station area types because institutions have much larger 
trade areas than urban and neighborhood centers. The bicycle net­
work must be complete and adequately provisioned in all directions 
and often along multiple parallel paths. At the University of New 
Mexico, for instance, campus occupies three quarters of a mile along 
Central Avenue and has a strong relationship with a satellite campus 
approximately one mile south. Multiple parallel north-south bicycle 
routes are necessary in addition to east-west routes serving down­
town and the rest of the Central Avenue corridor. 
NEIGHBORHOOD CENTER STATIONS	
[T] 
Neighborhood Center (NC) stations are another uncommon station 
type in Albuquerque; Solano is the local example. These stations are 
located at the portion of a neighborhood that is its most intensive 
in terms of density and mix of uses. Along the ART trajectory 
analyzed, neighborhood centers are located at Central Avenue, 
where the ART stations are. As such, station locations and centers 
align well. Other neighborhoods, such as those north and south of 
downtown, may be served by future transit in locations outside of 
the neighborhood center.
Stations in neighborhood centers primarily serve residents in their 
daily commutes and as they access major designations outside of 
their neighborhood, such as downtown, institutions, or the fair­
grounds. Neighborhood centers may also act as destinations where 
they have established and active main streets. Solano is an example 
of a neighborhood center that acts as a destination for people from 
outside of the neighborhood. Those neighborhood centers that 
attract outsiders are likely to run into issues between residents and 
the intensity of development in the center as parking and develop­
ment cause friction. The best means of alleviating this friction is to 
establish more neighborhood centers, connected by transit. 
NEIGHBORHOOD STATIONS 	
[T]
Neighborhood (N) stations were not studied in this process, howev­
er the type may be established in the future in Albuquerque. These 
stations serve neighborhoods but are not located in the neighbor­
hood center. Often this occurs where major corridors run along the 
edge of a neighborhood, and those corridors have either not been 
zoned commercial or they have but they do not support commer­
cial activity. Neighborhood stations do not serve as destinations for 
those outside of the neighborhood. Rather they are used primarily 
for commuting. These station types are not ideal unless they are sur­
rounded by very intense residential development.

SUBURBAN CENTER STATIONS 	
[S]
Suburban Center (SC) stations are generally uncommon, occurring 
where neighborhood structure contradicts the character of adjacent 
major corridors. These areas are close-in, meaning they are relatively 
near the city center such that the neighborhood fabric is made of 
gridded and interconnected streets. However they are suburban 
due to the character of development and zoning along adjacent 
corridors. Interestingly, this is a common type in Albuquerque. 
Yucca, Atrisco, Washington, San Mateo and Louisiana fall into this 
category; they have relatively traditional neighborhood fabric while 
the adjacent corridor has been developed with strip commercial and 
other heavily automobile-centric patterns.
These station areas are in need of significant redevelopment, but 
due to the scale of property ownership, they are not likely to move 
to the Transitional Station category. Transitional areas have the abil­
ity to be redeveloped on a large scale because property ownership 
along the corridor tends to be in large sites. The close-in Suburban 
Center Station then aims to become a neighborhood center but 
could become a larger and more intense town center if development 
is coordinated between many property owners.
EXURBAN CENTER STATIONS 	
[S]
Exurban Center (EC) stations present the greatest potential for 
return on investment from ART, according to the study by Center 
for Neighborhood Technology. These areas represent such potential 
because they tend to be very underdeveloped and have property 
ownership patterns that are very large under single ownership. 
The stations in the category in Albuquerque include Unser, Coors, 
Wyoming, Eubank, Juan Tabo and Tramway. These often include 
suburban shopping centers or warehouse areas. Shopping centers 
are the most common condition near these stations, a land use that 
is subject to regular redevelopment, typically on a 20 year cycle. 
Suburban shopping centers are often built inexpensively and the 
majority of the land area is given to surface parking lots. Recent 
trends in shopping center redevelopment have been to establish 
more intensive centers with retail and housing, which act as town 
centers for surrounding suburban areas.
Suburban and Exurban Town Center Station areas tend to lack a 
sense of place, following common patterns of single-use, segregated 
development. Stations in these areas can reduce overall traffic in 
two ways: capturing ridership for the transit system and capturing 
trips from the surrounding suburban residential areas by providing a 
number of destinations in a single location.
These stations act equally as destinations and sources of ridership. 
Ridership is built both by residents within the town center redevel­
opment and as mini park-and-rides providing a means of shifting 
from cars to transit for surrounding residential areas. Because these 
town center redevelopments tend to be large and service a very large 
surrounding suburban area, they attract and retain national retail 
tenants and grocers. Because these types of tenants are not located 
near the city center, suburban and exurban town center stations are 
destinations for residents of closer-in neighborhoods. 
SUBURBAN COMMUTER STATIONS 	
[S]
Suburban Commuter (SC) stations were not studied in this process, 
however the type is included further out on the ART system. These 
stations are purely utility, aimed at capturing drivers and moving 
them onto the transit system. This station type creates very little 
value and should generally be avoided. Suburban commuter stations 
are appropriate in suburban areas far outside of the city center, 
where the corridor they are located along is zoned purely residential 
with little likelihood for redevelopment. Instead of Suburban Com­
muter Stations, stations should be located instead where they are 
able to generate redevelopment and infill, creating value. g

to Robert Gibbs, author of The Principals

Another important asset for successful

high demand areas link University and Nob

within a five minute walk.

addressed throughout the corridor. Many

All of Central Avenue is zoned for commer­
cial, as are other major corridors throughout 
Albuquerque. This practice was common 
across the country with the advent of eu­
clidean zoning, based on uses instead of on 
the form of the built environment. The over 
abundance of available commercial land 
eliminated the land scarcity that encour­
ages more intense development along main 
streets. With miles and miles of commercial, 
low intensity commercial users proliferated. 
The resulting spread-out commercial corri­
dors required people to drive between busi­
nesses, and for those businesses to provide 
parking. The majority of Central Avenue is 
the result of this process: low intensity com­
mercial uses with a lot of parking.
Research has shown that retail streets only 
function successfully for short distances. 
Best practices today recommend retail 
streets be no longer than ¼ mile, up to ½ 
mile in rare cases. This is indirectly cor­
roborated by the highly-studied design of 
malls, which are typically based upon a ¼ 
mile length. Historic main streets follow a 
similar rule. In order to produce successful 
retail along Central Avenue, the portions of 
the corridor with commercial uses must be 
significantly reduced.

PARKING DEMAND
Achieving a balance between parking supply 
and development is a critical challenge in 
developing transit-oriented development 
(TOD). Denser mixed-use development 
sited around high quality transit increases 
the potential for multiple trips to be linked 
through alternative and active modes and 
fosters the type of urban form that creates 
a “park once” environment for motorists. 
In addition, TOD promotes self-selection, 
improving transit access for households 
who wish to be car free, or a one car family. 
Recent research of TOD projects around the 
United States suggests that TOD residents 
are twice as likely not to own a car as other 
households, while they are two to five times 
more likely to commute by transit than oth­
ers in their region. 
A recent study produced by Smart Growth 
America found that despite building less 
parking than recommended by the Institute 
of Transportation Engineers’ (ITE) Trip 
Generation and Parking Generation guides, 
TODs across the country were producing 
a ratio of parking demand between 58 and 
84 percent of actual supply provided. One 
reason for this is that TODs produce far 
fewer vehicle trips than standard trip genera­
tion models estimate. Furthermore, TODs 
produce lower rates of parking demand than 
what is estimated by traditional parking 
generation ratios because many sites share 
parking between commercial and residential 
uses, unbundle parking from residential 
rents, and charge market rates for commer­
cial parking. Despite reduced levels of de­
mand compared to traditional development, 
most TODs with residential and commer­
cial components require some amount of 
parking to achieve market feasibility and to 
secure project financing. 
The following strategies offer solutions to 
best manage existing parking supply and ac­
commodate growth and parking demand at 
TOD zones along the ART corridor.

DEMAND-BASED PRICING
Progressive parking policies manage a 
city’s existing supply of parking. A key 
component of any such policy is demand-
based pricing (also referred to as demand-
responsive pricing, variable-rate pricing, 
performance-based pricing, or dynamic 
pricing). Demand-based pricing charges the 
lowest possible rate that achieves availability 
targets, better aligning price and demand 
to ensure there is always an open parking 
space. 
Researchers have determined the ideal 
on-street parking occupancy rate to be 
around 85%, which leaves roughly one to 
two spaces available per block so that cars 
no longer have to circle the block to find 
parking. To achieve this, cities increase the 
cost of parking or reduce time limits in areas 
with higher occupancy rates, and decrease 
the cost of park­
ing or increase 
time limits in 
areas with occu­
pancy rates lower 
than 85%. The 
optimal occupancy rate for off-street facili­
ties is 90-95%.
Drivers are either bargain hunters or con­
venience seekers. Convenience seekers are 
more willing to pay for an available front 
door spot. Many shoppers and diners are 
typically less sensitive to parking charges 
because they stay for shorter periods of time 
than an employee or all-day visitors. Many 
longer-stay parkers find it worthwhile to 
walk a block to save on parking fees. With 
proper pricing, the bargain hunters will 
choose currently underutilized lots and 
more distant meters, leaving the prime 
spots free for those convenience seekers. For 
merchants, it is important to make prime 
spots available for these people: those who 
are willing to pay a fee to park are also those 
who are willing to spend money in stores 
and restaurants.

RESIDENTIAL PARKING PERMIT 
PROGRAM
A Residential Parking Permit (RPP) 
program operates by exempting permit­
ted vehicles from the parking restrictions 
and time limits for non-metered, on-street 
parking spaces within a geographic area. The 
primary goal is to manage parking spillover 
into residential neighborhoods. RPPs work 
best in neighborhoods that are impacted by 
high parking demand from other uses, such 
as:
+
+
Universities, colleges, neighborhood 
schools, or hospitals
+
+
Transit stations
+
+
Popular commercial, retail, entertain­
ment, tourist, or recreational destina­
tions
By managing spillover, RPPs can ensure 
that residential neighborhoods are not 
overwhelmed by commuters, employees, 
or visitors, thereby enabling local residents 
to park their vehicles on-street. Most RPP 
programs allow those without a permit to 
park for generally two to four hours during 
a specified period, such as 8:00 AM to 6:00 
PM, Monday to Friday. Permit holders are 
exempt from these regulations. Ownership 
of a permit, however, does not guarantee the 
availability of a parking space.
Throughout Albuquerque there are ap­
proximately 80 blocks spread amongst 30 
streets with existing RPP zones within three 
blocks of the ART corridor. The City allows 
for the establishment of an RPP zone if a 
study determines that a significant portion 
of on-street parking is being utilized by non-
residents of a residential street. The existing 
regulations allow for each address within 
an RPP zone to obtain up to three permits, 
and a permit per unit for lots or addresses 
with multiple units. Permits are issued to 
residents free of charge. Unlike RPP Zones 
in most cities, some RPP Zones along the 
ART corridor, such as the U Zone, are in ef­
fect during the daytime in order to mitigate

SHARED PARKING
S
hared parking agreements 
are arrangements with pri­
vate parking lot owners that 
provide for privately owned 
off-street parking to be available to 
the public during specified periods 
of time, usually when the parking 
lot is in low demand for its associ­
ated tenants.
A typical example of shared parking would 
be a land use that creates parking demand 
during the day, which could then become 
available to the public during non-business 
hours or at other times when there is an 
overabundance of available parking. The 
agreement with the parking lot owner 
would stipulate the times during which 
public users may park in the lot and terms 
for compensation and operation. 
 A.22 
The City could enter into three types of 
agreements with a private property owner. 
These are:
1.	 Leasing of a private lot: Under this 
arrangement, parking spaces would es­
sentially be “rented” from the property 
owner and the City would be entitled 
to establish regulations during “shared” 
use hours. Upgrades (lighting, striping, 
signage, etc.) could be made and the 
City would enforce compliance with 
regulations.
2.	 Private ownership, public enforce­
ment: Under this arrangement the 
private property owner would open 
their lot to the public and establish 
regulations (including any pricing). The 
owner could choose to charge for park­
ing, depending on parking demand. 
The City would enforce compliance 
with regulations and collect citation 
revenue. 
3.	 Third-party management: It may be 
simpler and more cost-effective for the 
City to contract with a private com­
pany with experience facilitating shared 
parking arrangements instead of creat­
ing and managing its own agreements. 
This company would also establish 
regulations (including any pricing).

VALET PARKING
Municipal valet services may be used to 
manage spikes in parking demand in busy 
commercial districts. These services offer an 
easy alternative to finding on-street parking 
by allowing drivers to drop-off their car at 
one location, shop, dine, and run errands, 
and then pick up their car when they are 
done. Unlike other valets that serve only 
one business, municipal valets are open to 
the public. With just a few on-street spots 
at each valet location, municipalities can 
maximize their existing parking supply by 
having the valet use underutilized parking 
lots and garages.
Valet parking provides an opportunity to 
shift demand to off-street lots and increase 
the ease of parking for visitors to high-de­
mand areas. It can also increase the effective 
parking supply through more efficient use, 
as valet operators can tandem or triple park 
vehicles. Valet parking is useful in evenings 
and during seasonal or event peaks, particu­
larly to support restaurants and the theater. 
Typically, these programs can be funded 
through neighborhood business associations 
or PBDs. In general, cities or businesses 
enter into a contract with one or more valet 
operators to provide the service. In order 
to make valet services a single, seamless 
operation, consistent branding (signage and 
uniform) should be required.
 A.23 
Pilot municipal valet services in strategic 
neighborhoods like Downtown and Nob 
Hill. Selection of valet parking drop-off 
locations need to consider how the loss of 
on-street parking spaces will affect parking 
behavior and whether drop-off and pick-
up would negatively affect traffic flow and 
street operations. These locations could be 
coordinated with existing passenger loading 
zones to minimize parking loss.

with historic and new office buildings, ho­
tels, cultural centers, and a growing number 
of dwellings. The wonderful urban street 
grid is the structure to create a more vibrant 
and active downtown, with redevelopment 
opportunities including a number of surface 
parking lots. Residential is slowly coming 
back downtown and as a result a grocery 
store recently opened. The area along Cen­
tral Avenue is higher intensity while just a 
few lots away there are historic single family 
homes. The neighborhood has a great urban 
vibe with convenient access to the rest of 
Albuquerque.

PARKING ASSESSMENT
Analysis of existing parking supply reveals 
that on-street spaces are underutilized, with 
higher rates of utilization at off-street facili­
ties. Of 18 sub-study areas surveyed in the 
2016 Downtown Parking Study, on-street 
parking occupancy surpassed the optimal 
rate of 85% in just two areas and during 
three hours of data collection. Of 14 sub-
study areas with off-street parking, parking 
occupancy surpassed the optimal rate of 
90% in just one area observed during a sin­
gle hour of data collection. When account­
ing for public off-street supply only, just one 
sub-study area revealed occupancy above 
90% (93%), during one hour of data collec­
tion. This evidence suggests that Downtown 
parking supply is currently underutilized for 
a central business district. 
In the in-depth 2014 study Albuquerque, 
New Mexico Downtown Walkability Analysis, 
Jeff Speck notes that many streets in down­
town Albuquerque have lost a significant 
share of on-street parking due to travel lanes 
that are either too wide or too numerous 
given the volumes of traffic that they handle 
to accommodate standard parallel park­
ing stalls. On some streets, where on-street 
spaces are non-existent, there is adequate 
space to add new parking stalls without rear­
ranging lane configurations. By restriping 
certain streets, new on-street parking could 
be produced through existing infrastructure 
at a rate that would cost millions of dollars 
to replicate if built as new parking struc­
tures.
Parking concerns in Downtown related to 
TOD will likely be related to a perceived 
loss or scarcity of parking that may not cor­
relate to actual rates of utilization. In order 
to receive the greatest return on investment 
from ART in this area, it is critical that 
strategies focus on efficiently distributing 
parking demand to underutilized facilities, 
leveraging opportunities for shared parking 
partnerships, and integration of parking into 
districtwide mobility branding and com­
munications.

NEIGHBORHOODS
Downtown stretches from Coal to Lomas, 
between 8th Street and the train tracks,  
or about 250 acres. The area is about 200 
acres excluding the Wells Park neighbor­
hood transition to the northwest. The 300 
foot square block size in downtown is rare 
in the region, and a distinct advantage that 
supports walkability. However downtown 
has significantly deteriorated, as many US 
downtowns have. Block after block is oc­
cupied by surface parking lots and many 
more recent buildings built downtown are 
out of character for their location and do 
not activate the street. However, some very 
recent developments and projects in the 
design stage are moving in a good direction 
to revive and reconnect the district.
In terms of housing, downtown is still defi­
cient, even with the new options provided 
over the last decade. The structure of the 
district can easily support thousands of 
new housing units, which are necessary to 
activate a downtown into a 24-hour com­
munity. Currently a four-block section of 
Central Avenue is the sole active pedestrian 
area. Infill housing is necessary to support 
a greater diversity of retail offerings and 
expand the district.
North-south pedestrian connections via cross­
walks along Central is critical to connect neigh­
bors to the employment destinations in the north 
and civic destinations in the south.
To the south, west, and northwest of down­
town are a series of historic neighborhoods, 
very well connected to downtown. These 
provide a significant amount of nearby 
housing, but at present it is not comfortable 
or convenient to walk or bike from these ar­
eas to downtown. Similarly, the train tracks 
disconnect the neighborhoods of EDo. g

STREET TREES ARE NEEDED 
THROUGHOUT DOWNTOWN.
With few exceptions, Downtown’s streets 
lack street trees. Street trees play a vital role 
in the walkability and livability of districts 
and neighborhoods. Physically, they provide 
shade, in an environment where shade is 
important. Psychologically, trees play a vital 
role in human wellness. Trees are just as 
important to downtown streets as they are 
to neighborhood streets.
The arid climate is often brought up as a 
reason not to plant street trees. However 
there are trees more well adapted to the high 
desert that work well as street trees. With­
out street trees, especially in a downtown, 
sun has a heat island effect, creating a high 
temperature microclimate.

 B.03
DOWNTOWN STREETS ARE PRI­
ORITIZED FOR CARS, INHIBITING 
PEDESTRIAN ACTIVITY
Over time, as Downtown activities re­
quired more automobile access and parking 
and people moved out of downtown and 
adjacent neighborhoods, the transportation 
system was revised to service the increas­
ing use of cars. The result is a downtown 
area with too much space dedicated to the 
automotive mode of transportation, and not 
enough reserved for pedestrians and cyclists. 
This condition is common throughout the 
US. In most urban areas, traffic counts have 
spiked and are reducing, being replaced by 
walking, biking, and transit use. Vehicle ca­
pacity is excessive for present and projected 
future usage.
When too much space is given to auto­
mobiles and too little to pedestrians and 
bicycles, walking and cycling is uncomfort­
able and often dangerous. Wide travel lanes 
contribute to speeding and speeding makes 
streets unsafe for all users. Even parallel 
parking spaces that are too wide can con­
tribute to speeding. Lane width given to cars 
has often resulted in a loss of street trees, 
pedestrian-scaled lighting, and sidewalks 
with ample width for two or more people.
 A.03 
+
+
Restripe streets to reduce travel lane 
widths and provide for additional 
bicycle and pedestrian facilities, or 
on-street parking where it is missing. 
Reduce travel lane dimensions to 10 
feet wide in general, or 11 feet wide 
along bus routes.

turn radii threaten pedestrians in crosswalks. 
Cyclists are similarly affected overall by 
bike lanes directly adjacent to travel lanes. 
Recently the bike lanes on Lead, Coal, and 
MLK have been provided with buffers from 
travel lanes to increase safety.

SUMMARY
The University District has a long and var­
ied history, common to residential districts 
adjacent to Universities and large employers. 
The area employers have been automobile 
oriented for much of this time, consolidated 
within inward-facing campuses. The neigh­
borhood has fared relatively well through 
the influx of transient student populations, 
but many of its current issues are related: 
lack of sufficient property maintenance, 
parking in the wrong locations, illegal 
housing unit conversions, open spaces and 
parking areas unsupervised at night. The 
neighborhood itself, split into more than 
one distinct entity, has advocated for many 
reasonable changes over decades, having 
seen few of those changes happen. The 
area also suffers from large land users - the 
employers, schools, and grave yard - which 
concentrate traffic on to a limited number 
of north-south streets. With many students 
and residents in the area, safe walking and 
cycling routes are needed.
ART can mark the beginning of a new 
chapter. Along with this transit investment, 
UNM is also focused on a new identity. As 
they move towards a primarily residential 
school, rather than a commuter school, 
neighborhood dynamics may shift. In their 
new image, UNM can refocus on the edge 
of their campus and its interface with the 
City. Their investment in the new Physical 
and Astronomy Interdisciplinary Science 
Building at Yale Park marks such a shift. The 
resurgent commercial core at Harvard Street 
can be bolstered and grow into a small dis­
trict that supports both the University and 
the neighborhood. Redevelopment along 
Central Avenue can be focused on increas­
ing the residential population and address­
ing long standing parking issues. Neighbor­
hood beautification efforts can be addressed, 
helping increase transit ridership.

NEIGHBORHOODS
The station area south of UNM includes 
two neighborhoods: University Heights and 
Silver Hill. Both neighborhoods have active 
associations and have advocated for very rea­
sonable changes since at least 1978. While 
a few of those changes have taken place, 
many have not and will re-appear later in 
this section.
UNIVERSITY HEIGHTS is located 
south of Central Avenue and east of Yale 
Boulevard. The neighborhood is typified by 
a varied mix of housing types and density, 
a pattern historic to the district. For a time, 
the neighborhood had significant issues 
with student housing intrusion, which has 
receded in recent years. While higher inten­
sity development has been permitted in the 
neighborhood historically, recent experienc­
es include illegal unit additions to existing 
buildings. These illegal additions are often 
parked within former front yards, contribut­
ing to a lack of public realm safety. Similarly 
as a largely rental neighborhood, there is 
little maintenance of park strips, which have 
become treeless and bare in most instances, 
reducing walkability.
SILVER HILL is located south of Central 
Avenue and west of Yale Boulevard. The 
neighborhood is well known for its historic 
district, the pinnacle of which is the sec­
tion of Silver Avenue with a median. The 
neighborhood is comprised of primarily 
single family houses with some multi-family 
buildings, both historic and new. Student 
housing has been less prevalent in Silver Hill 
than University Heights. University Boule­
vard currently splits the neighborhood into 
two sections, because of its high speeds and 
six-travel-lane section. There is some historic 
retail in Silver Hill along Central Avenue, 
but much of this has deteriorated over time.
SPRUCE PARK is north of Central and 
west of University, but was outside of the 
planning area for this process. It is a lower 
density, largely single-family detached 
neighborhood with some multi-family and 
mixed-use along MLK Jr. Avenue.

NOB HILL
SUMMARY
Nob Hill has two distinct zones: Lower and 
Upper Nob Hill and while their redevelop­
ment potential varies, their goals are aligned. 
Nob Hill wants to preserve its historic 
character and unique attributes, enhance its 
retail viability, and sensitively accommodate 
the anticipated new residential density as a 
result of ART. Nob Hill is distinguished by a 
variety of historic, characteristic and special, 
(even if non-contributing) buildings. With a 
greater concentration of buildings worthy of 
retaining, Lower Nob Hill should focus on 
preservation goals. With more empty parcels 
and properties likely to develop, Upper Nob 
Hill should focus on redevelopment goals.

NETWORKS
Nob Hill is a collection of early 20th cen­
tury developments on either side of Central 
Avenue. A few of these diverge from the 
standard block layout of Albuquerque’s early 
neighborhood grids. Between Girard and 
Carlisle Boulevards, north of Central Ave­
nue, the grid shifts in a few interesting ways. 
This shift creates Monte Vista Boulevard, 
which intersects with Central Avenue and 
Girard Boulevard in a problematic 5-way in­
tersection. Additional, less substantial shifts 
occur further east throughout the neighbor­
hood. These grid shifts limit the directness 
of thru-streets, creating calmer streets for 
low to medium density housing but fewer 
direct vehicular connections. To the west, 
part of the grid is disrupted by the Univer­
sity, however this does not have a significant 
effect in Nob Hill. Because of the grid shifts 
and University, vehicular traffic east-west 
is pushed to major roads, increasing their 
volumes while limiting traffic through the 
neighborhood.
Nob Hill has a relatively complete east-
west bicycle network south of Central, but 
is less complete to the north, due in part 
to disconnection at the University. In the 
north-south direction, the area lacks regular 
bicycle connections. The pattern of existing 
and proposed bicycle facilities creates an 
incomplete network with a series of major 
gaps.

5-WAY TO 4-WAY INTERSECTION
Currently Central Avenue, Girard Boule­
vard, and Mote Vista Boulevard meet at 
a 5-way signalized intersection in Lower 
Nob Hill. The intersection can create hectic 
conditions, resulting in travel delays, and 
presents dangerous conflicts between all 
road users. 
Between 2010 and 2013, 99 collisions oc­
curred at this intersection, including three 
collisions involving bicyclists. This accounts 
for over eight percent of collisions through­
out the Nob Hill study area during this 
period. 
Re imagining this intersection as a 4-way 
intersection by closing off Monte Vista Bou­
levard as designed, would increase safety due 
to a decreased number of potential conflict 
points, facilitate the development of public 
space and pedestrian oriented developments. 
In addition, it would bring congestion 
relief by simplifying the operations at the 
intersection. Whereas a 5-leg intersection 
assigns approximately 20 seconds of time 
for throughput in each direction (over four 
phases), a 4-leg intersection only has two 
phases, each at approximately 43 seconds, 
facilitating the flow of traffic through the 
intersection.
The closing of a 5-way intersection has been 
accomplished successfully in other cities, 
such as Los Angeles and Atlanta, shown to 
the right. In the case of Los Angeles, the 
closure prevents potential conflicts between 
pedestrians and vehicles as vehicles would 
previously enter a residential street at rela­
tively high speeds, while creating a commu­
nity space. In Atlanta, pedestrian activity is 
fostered within the active Midtown District.

 B.03
HEIGHT STANDARDS IN THE IDO 
WILL ENCOURAGE ILL-PROPOR­
TIONED BUILDINGS.
Most zoning codes restrict height in feet, 
such as a maximum of 54 feet. There is 
another system used that measures height 
in the number of stories. Measuring height 
in feet has a number of problems: develop­
ers are encouraged to fit as many stories as 
possible within the height limit, resulting in 
low ceiling heights. It discourages appropri­
ately scaled ground floor retail spaces which 
should be between 16 and 20 feet tall. It 
results in monotonous streets where every 
building is the same height. 
When height is restricted by stories rather 
than feet, it allows developers to determine 
the height of each story according to its 
use and market. Apartment ceiling height, 
for instance, can vary depending upon 
the target market of the unit. Limits can 
carefully control this system so outcomes 
are still predictable and within a reasonable 
range. Historically, many cities developed 
in this manner, resulting in the variety that 
articulation requirements attempt to create.

LOUISIANA BOULEVARD
STATION AREA
The EXPO New Mexico site is a city- and

However, activity on the site is infrequent. 
With the majority of the EXPO New

vehicular, bicycles, and pedestrian access 
through the area is restricted. 
As with other districts on the Central cor­
ridor, the International District is rich and 
diverse in culture. Yet these aspects are not 
fully expressed in the urban structure and

nificantly underutilized asset that currently 
separates rather than connects the various 
neighborhoods. There are no clear neighbor­
hood centers (despite the popularity of Talin 
Market) and little neighborhood structure. 
These issues notwithstanding, the State Fair, 
Casino, and Flea Market functions are antic­
ipated to remain on the EXPO New Mexico 
site, while a re-envisioning the Fairgrounds 
as a "New Mexico Commons” has been 
proposed by some community members.
Last but not least, given that the residential 
neighborhoods surrounding the Fairgrounds 
are fairly well-established, transit-related 
development opportunities are mostly 
along the southern edge of the Fairgrounds 
on Central, as well as on sites fronting the 
Fairgrounds along Central, Louisiana, San 
Pedro, and Lomas. Currently many vacant 
or underutilized sites provide an opportu­
nity for redevelopment, especially around 
the Louisiana Boulevard station, which can 
serve as a center for the adjacent neighbor­
hoods and support activities at the EXPO 
New Mexico site.

IMPACT OF TRANSIT
To call Albuquerque a unique city is an understatement. New Mexico’s culture and natural environment is 
not replicated elsewhere in the United States. As the state’s largest city and economic center, Albuquerque 
has many unique assets. However, Albuquerque shares similar challenges with countless cities across the 
American Sunbelt. To combat these challenges, such as cost of providing services, traffic congestion and ac­
cess to economic opportunity, investments in multi-modal transportation has broken ground along the Cen­
tral Avenue corridor. The Albuquerque Rapid Transit line will undoubtedly encourage new higher-density, 
walkable, mixed-use development within station areas. Albuquerque and Bernalillo County will be amongst 
the primary benefactors of new development and its additional tax production. What type of property tax 
revenue can the community expect to receive after ART is completed?
This section addresses the financial impact of dense development 
and the benefits of access to transit. Property tax data was utilitzed 
and the 3D value per acre model used as a foundation to forecast 
future property tax production after the completion of the ART line 
along Central Avenue.
The results of the analysis were clear. Unassuming, incremental 
residential developments were shown to generate potent tax value to 
the community. Two and three-story townhome/condominium de­
velopments in the Central Avenue corridor proved to produce more 
than 10-15 times the property tax revenue of a standard big box on 
a per-acre basis. Perhaps even more encouraging for the future of 
ART and its planning efforts is the amount of value already added 
within the corridor over the last 10 years, which outpaces the rest of 
the City. It is estimated that after full buildout (15-20 years), rede­
velopment nodes along the corridor will produce $2.8M - $15M in 
total annual property tax production.
The foundation of the analysis is the value per acre method. Utiliz­
ing property tax data from Bernalillo County, property tax values 
of each parcel were processed, analyzed and visualized in a 3D Tax 
Value per Acre model. Taking into account how much land a par­
ticular development consumes enables different types of properties, 
in different parts of the community to be compared in an apples to 
apples manner. The more land a parcel consumes, the more public 
services it requires (streets, utilities, etc.).

resulting in a total corridor market value of 
$9,032,805,989. This is an overall incre­
ment amounting to 69 percent of current 
market value. 
This conservatively assumed no increase 
or change (e.g. from depreciation of non-
redevelopment property) over the projected 
periods. Under this scenario, if full develop­
ment can be accomplished within 10 years, 
the annual increase in market value of prop­
erty would be 6.9 percent. If build out occurs 
over 20 years, the rate drops to 3.5 percent.
Not all areas of Central Avenue will achieve 
the same levels of development intensity. 
Referring to the analysis by Urban3 of po­
tential development intensity and value in a 
constrained area adjacent to Central Avenue 
within the Nob Hill community, it would 
be misleading to suggest that the IDO 
would result in equivalent high potential 
value-per-acre across the entire 15-mile cor­
ridor. However, because the total inventory 
of potentially re-developable land is larger in 
the Suburban station areas than in either the 
Urban or Transitional areas, a larger poten­
tial increase in aggregate value could occur. 
While the station areas and neighborhoods 
further to the east and west are of overall 
lower density and activity levels now than is 
the case with station areas Downtown and 
in nearby neighborhoods, there is consider­
ably more land susceptible to redevelopment 
in those currently lower density areas to the 
east and to the west.  
On a sensitivity analysis basis, over the full 
15 mile corridor, compared with the prior 
zoning, if the IDO entitles two times the 
development intensity allowable under prior 
zoning, this would result in an incremen­
tal market valuation of $7.38 billion, an 
incremental assessed value of $2.46 billion, 
and a tax increment yield from the combi­
nation of increased property taxes and Gross 
Receipts Taxes of $86.2 million annually 
or $7.18 million monthly. This amount 
could amortize a total of $1.71 billion at a 
3 percent interest rate, or $1.325 billion at 
5 percent. 
If the rate of increase in valuation implied 
by the Nob Hill analysis is representative, 
then 4 times the development intensity pre­
viously allowed, all other things being equal, 
would double those amounts yet again.
The recent update to population and jobs 
projections suggests that prudence should 
be exercised in making these calculations. 
Neighborhoods with strong potential de­
mand could experience rapid development, 
and in those cases a 10 year build out may 
be feasible. Absent growth in the regional 
employment base it may take 20 years over­
all to achieve Central Avenue infill potential. 
One additional barrier for this action is the 
lack of GRT information on a parcel-specif­
ic basis from the State, without which it is 
challenging to administer a TIDD meaning­
fully. The TIDD basis for housing, retail, 
commercial is important to be backed up by 
an actuarial study to project the actual GRT 
per area along the corridor.

CENTRAL AVENUE TIDD
The City can, under current law, establish 
a Central Avenue TIDD to set the table for

to further the City’s goals associated with 
better transportation choices and station

That second half could also be structured 
to favor projects which can further attract 
matching funds various grant-making and

available funds compared to simply spend­
ing down the bond proceeds. 
It might be branded as the Central Avenue 
Community Benefits Bank, and it could be 
replicated as advanced transportation ser­
vices are programmed along the other two 
Premium Transit Corridors, Paseo del Norte 
and University/I25/Jefferson. 
The City should form a working group to 
further explore and recommend specifica­
tions for this financing source.

FINANCE 
ACTION 03
Create mechanisms for effective land assem­
bly, which may include land trusts, and land 
banks to reactivate underutilized properties. 
The availability of land along Central Av­
enue for higher density, mixed use, walkable 
urbanism is significant, assuming half of 
surface parking is available for development.
Properties can become vacant for many rea­
sons: abandonment, demolition of previous 
buildings, tax liens resulting from delin­
quency and non-payment, and tax foreclo­
sure. Property may have been purchased for 
public improvements or for buildings that 
was never utilized, or legacy public facilities 
such as schools may have been subject to 
consolidation, among other reasons. 
Many cities and counties nationally have 
created programs to ease disposition and 
sometimes assembly for redevelopment of 
vacant properties. In some states, the right 
for local government to enter “non cash 
bids” for such properties is a mechanism 
intended to facilitate property reuse. Prop­
erty taxation and disposition of property 
is handled by the State of New Mexico 
through the New Mexico Taxation and Rev­
enue Department and by Bernalillo County. 
Since volumes of available vacant properties 
are large and because of the complications 
of land assembly, various jurisdictions and 
organizations have developed specialized 
programs to aid in this task.
LAND BANKS
A land bank acquires, holds, and transfers 
interest in real property to promote rede­
velopment and reuse of vacant, abandoned, 
foreclosed or tax-delinquent properties; sup­
ports targeted efforts to stabilize neighbor­
hoods; and stimulates residential, commer­
cial and industrial development, all in ways 
that are consistent with goals and priorities 
established by local government partners 
and other community stakeholders. 
Working independently with the request of 
municipal and other partners, land banks 
acquire properties, liens, notes or deeds 
through purchase, donation, forfeiture and 
other transfers. Land banks will hold title 
and maintain properties tax free, will extin­
guish delinquent taxes and liens as permit­
ted by law, and, in some cases, demolish/
deconstruct buildings, with the intent of 
preparing property for conveyance back to 
the market or to other uses. Land banks will 
also lease/adopt interim uses for properties 
as necessary and appropriate.  
LAND TRUSTS
Land trusts are mechanisms that can provide 
for the separate ownership of land from 
the buildings on top of that land, result­
ing in increased stability of ownership and 
community character over time (see http://
community-wealth.org/strategies/panel/
clts/index.html). Over 120 such organiza­
tions, non- profit organizations which have 
Section 501c3 federal tax exemptions, are 
members of the National Community Land 
Trust Network which joined forces this year 
with Cornerstone Partnership to form the 
Grounded Solutions Network. See www.
citnetwork.org. 
There is one urban community land trust 
in Albuquerque, the Sawmill Community 
Land Trust, covering 27 acres north of 
Uptown and south of I-40. The Trust is 
described at http://www.sawmillclt.org/ and 
a case study is at http://albuquerque.about.
com/od/neighborhoods/i/landtrusts.htm .
Other New Mexico Community Land 
Trusts include the La Mesa and Mil Abrazos 
CLTs in Albuquerque; the Santa Fe Com­
munity Housing Trust in Santa Fe, and the 
Tierra Madre in Sunland Park, in Dona Ana 
County. These are all described with contact 
information at http://www.centerforneweco­
nomics.org/content/community-land-trusts-
directory.
IMPACT INVESTING PROVIDES 
NEW RESOURCES FOR DOING 
WELL AND DOING GOOD
During the last two years, there has been 
tremendous interest nationally on the 
part of foundations and socially-screened 
investment funds in impact investing. For 
example, the Ford Foundation announced 
that it would devote 12 percent of its assets 
or $1 billion toward impact investments. A 
new association, the Mission Investors Ex­
change, has been created to serve as a forum 
for this investment practice as well as to 
highlight specific investment opportunities. 
Summaries by leading investor/practitioners 
are published monthly in the Stanford So­
cial Impact Review in a new series, Mission 
Possible. Impact investing is also known as 
“pay for success” financing. The basic idea is 
to “purchase” the outcome desired, defined 
by a pre-determined set of performance 
measures.
DOUBLE BOTTOM LINE INVEST­
MENT FUNDS
Similarly, in the late 1990s, the Bay Area 
Family of Funds was created to provide such 
resources to help address poverty in that 
region’s 42 communities which exhibited 
persistent poverty. Investments could ad­
dress such opportunities as transit oriented 
development, small business development 
and land recycling. $200 million was raised 
from banks, corporations, foundations 
and government for this purpose. Three 
investment funds were managed by SEC 
registered investment advisors, who in turn 
agreed to use “double bottom line” term 
sheets which addressed both profitability 
and a set of community-defined benefits. 
Inspired by this effort and an earlier one in 
Massachusetts dedicated to creative financ­
ing of affordable housing, some $5 billion 
was invested through such funds in the early 
2000s across the United States.

Financial instruments are better

urban development patterns. This

Achieving the opportunity associated with

year period will result in the design, permit­

There is relatively little local experience with 
developing, investing in, and approving

from depository financial institutions such

constrained since the recession. Lenders are 
dependent upon secondary market purchas­

and advance funds provided by the 11 Dis­

insurance provided by the Federal Housing 
Administration. There are limits on how 
much space in a mixed use building can be 
commercial if purchased by Fannie Mae 
or Freddie Mac or insured by FHA which 
result in minimum heights of 4, 5 or 10 
stories respectively. FHA and Freddie Mac 
have pilot programs intended to lower these 
heights and restriction which can address 
this barrier.
